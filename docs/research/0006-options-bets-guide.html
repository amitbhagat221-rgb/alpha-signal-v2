Part 1 · The crash course

The one idea: an option price is a forecast

Every option price contains the market's forecast of how much the price will move. You make money only when you disagree with that forecast and turn out right.

This is why people are right about direction and still lose money on options. Being right is not enough. You have to be more right than the price already assumed.

A real example: the RBI decision on 7 October 2026

On the evening of 6 October, NIFTY closed at 22,776. The 22,800 call cost 125.75 points and the 22,800 put cost 179. Buying both (a straddle) cost 304.75 points, which is 1.34% of the index. That 1.34% is the market's forecast of how far NIFTY would move by the 13 October expiry.

What the price forecast
± 1.34%

The straddle's cost as a share of the index, for a week.

What happened on the day
−0.76%

RBI raised rates by 0.25%, the first hike in four years. NIFTY fell 0.76%.

Who was right
Neither, yet

Someone who bet "down" with a put spread made 49%. The straddle buyer made only 4%. The move was real, but smaller than the price had already assumed.

The rule of 16: turning VIX into an expected move

India VIX is the market's forecast of NIFTY's yearly movement, in percent. There are about 256 trading days a year, and √256 = 16. So VIX ÷ 16 ≈ the typical daily move. Divide by about 7.2 for a week and 3.5 for a month. About two days in three should land inside that range if markets were "normal". Try it:

The rule uses √252 ≈ 15.9 in the calculator; 16 is the mental shortcut. Real markets break the "two days in three" assumption in a specific way, shown in part 3.

Seven bets hide inside every position

Any option position, however many legs, is some mix of these seven bets. Professionals describe a trade by which bets it takes, not by its name. Traders call these "the Greeks"; the plain-English question comes first here, the Greek name in grey.

Bet 1

Direction

Do I make money if the market goes up, or down?

Delta. A bought call is +, a bought put is −, a straddle starts near 0.

Bet 2

Size of the move

Do I want a big move in either direction, or a quiet market?

Gamma. Option buyers want movement; option sellers want calm.

Bet 3

Price of options

Do I gain if options become more expensive (fear rises), or cheaper?

Vega. Buyers gain when implied volatility rises, even if the index does not move.

Bet 4

Time

Does each passing day pay me, or cost me?

Theta. The mirror image of bet 2: whoever wants movement pays rent each day it does not come.

Bet 5

Shape (skew)

Is downside protection priced too high or too low compared with upside bets?

Skew. On NIFTY, puts usually cost more than equally distant calls.

Bet 6

Calendar (term structure)

Is next week priced too high or too low compared with next month?

Term structure. Bought by calendar and diagonal spreads.

Bet 7

Togetherness (correlation)

Will the stocks in an index move together, or each go their own way?

Correlation. Traded by dispersion: index options against stock options.

The two that matter most are linked. Bet 2 (size of move) and bet 4 (time) are two sides of one coin. If you own options, you pay a little every day (time) for the right to profit from a big move. If you sell options, you collect that daily rent and accept the risk of the big move. Almost every option strategy is a choice about which side of this coin to stand on, and at what price.

80%

The whole of options trading in one sentence: options are usually overpriced, so sellers earn a steady premium, but the market's worst days are much worse than the price assumes, so a seller who cannot survive those days eventually loses everything. Buyers lose a little most days and can win big on the rare ones. Who comes out ahead depends on the size of the premium against the size and frequency of the surprises, and on surviving long enough to collect.

Choosing a trade: start from what you believe

A professional never starts with "I want to trade a condor". They start with a belief that differs from the price, then pick the cheapest structure that expresses only that belief. Work down the questions.

1 · Do I disagree with the market about direction?And how strongly, and by when?
Strong view, big move, soon: buy a call or put.
Moderate view, or options look expensive: a vertical spread (buy one, sell a further one to cut the cost).
Mild view, want income: sell a put below the market (if bullish) or a covered call.
2 · Do I disagree about how much it will move?Compare your expected move with the straddle price or VIX ÷ 16.
Bigger than priced: buy a straddle or strangle.
Smaller than priced: sell a strangle, an iron condor (capped) or an iron fly.
It will end near one level: a butterfly.
3 · Do I disagree about when?Is the near-term option priced differently from the later one?
Near-term too expensive (for example before an event): calendar spread, sell near, buy far.
Event is not priced enough: buy the expiry that contains the event.
4 · Do I disagree about the shape?Are downside puts too dear or too cheap compared with calls?
Puts too expensive: risk reversal (sell put, buy call) or a put ratio spread.
Crash protection too cheap: buy far out-of-the-money puts.
5 · Do I just want to change my portfolio's risk?No view on the option price at all.
Protect stocks I own: protective put or collar.
Earn income on stocks I own: covered call.
6 · If no answer is "yes", do not trade options.Without a disagreement with the price, you are paying costs to take a fair bet.
At ₹54.91 per round trip on one NIFTY lot at ₹50 premium, costs alone are 1.7% of premium. A fair bet minus costs is a losing bet.

How people actually make millions in options

  • Volume times a tiny edge. Market makers earn a fraction of a rupee per contract, millions of times a day, and hedge away the risk. This is where most of the money sits, and it needs speed and scale.
  • Patience with a cheap, convex bet. Universa and Ackman paid small premiums for years or weeks, and were paid many times over when the crash came (Ackman: $27 million became $2.6 billion in three weeks). The skill is staying in through the losing years.
  • Steady premium with strict sizing. Insurance sellers compound small gains; the ones who survive are the ones who size for the worst day, not the average day.
  • Being early on a mispricing nobody else is watching. Dispersion and skew trades pay well until they become crowded.

What almost never makes millions: buying short-dated calls and puts on a hunch. That is what about 97% of Indian individual traders do (SEBI FY26, via a secondary summary), and about 88% of them lose.

Part 4 · The professionals

Ten rules professionals live by

Know your bet before your strategy.

Write down which of the seven bets you are taking and why the price is wrong. If you cannot, do not trade.

Compare with the price, not with nothing.

"NIFTY will move 1%" is not a view. "NIFTY will move more than the 1.34% the straddle prices" is.

Use the rule of 16.

VIX ÷ 16 is the daily move priced in. At VIX 14 that is about 0.9%, or about 200 NIFTY points at 22,500.

Most days are quieter than priced.

79% of NIFTY days moved less than one expected move. That is the steady income for sellers.

The worst days are far worse than priced.

Four NIFTY days since 2015 moved more than four expected moves; a bell curve allows one in 60 years. Size for those days.

Size for survival.

The project's rule: at most one-third of capital as margin, so the worst historical week costs 12 to 15% of capital, not all of it.

Every leg costs money.

A four-leg trade has eight orders round trip: 8 × ₹20 brokerage plus 18% GST is about ₹189 per lot before taxes and slippage.

Caps are not free.

Buying protection 1% beyond sold strikes turned the project's winning strangle into a loser, with a worst week of −95% of margin.

Visible events are already priced.

The project tested skipping the strangle before RBI, Budget and election days. The skipped weeks would have paid more, not less.

Trade only what trades.

Use strikes with real volume. In stock options, exit two days before expiry to avoid physical delivery margins.

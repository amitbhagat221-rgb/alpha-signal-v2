Who wins in options, and how

The professionals who make serious money in options run a handful of businesses: making markets, selling insurance, buying insurance, trading one volatility against another, and exploiting how the market is built. This page explains each one with real numbers, and shows where this project fits.

Five ways professionals make money in options

Almost every successful options business is one of these five, or a mix. They differ in what they need: speed, capital, patience, or nerve.

1 · Making the market

Steadiest and biggest profitsJane Street · Citadel Securities · Optiver · IMC · SIG · HRT

What they do. Quote a price to buy and a slightly higher price to sell on thousands of option contracts at once, all day. Every time someone trades with them they earn the small gap between those prices. Then they immediately hedge the risk away by trading the index, futures or other options.

Why it makes millions. The gap is tiny, often a fraction of a rupee, but they do it millions of times a day. They are not betting on direction; they are a toll booth. Citadel Securities says it handles about 30% of all US listed option trades.

What it takes. Microsecond technology, an exchange membership, the lowest fees, a pricing model for every option, and billions in capital. Not something an individual can copy.

2 · Selling insurance (short volatility)

Steady gains, rare large lossesOption-income funds · put-writing funds · many prop desks · this project's paper trade

What they do. Sell options, usually out-of-the-money puts or calls, and collect the premium. Most options expire worthless, so most months they keep it.

Why it works. Buyers pay a little more than options are "worth" on average, because people pay extra to be protected, the way insurance premiums exceed average claims. That extra is called the volatility risk premium.

The danger. A rare crash wipes out years of premium. LJM lost over 80% in three days in February 2018; XIV lost 96% in one evening. The survivors size small and cap their losses.

How big it is. Option-based ETFs in the US held about $245 billion at end-2025. The biggest, JPMorgan's JEPI and JEPQ, held over $60 billion together, mostly by selling calls on stocks they own.

3 · Buying insurance (long volatility and tail hedging)

Small losses most years, huge gains in crashesUniversa · tail-risk funds · Ackman 2020

What they do. The opposite of model 2. Buy far-out-of-the-money puts, usually on the S&P 500, and keep buying them every month even though most expire worthless.

How they make millions. In a crash those cheap puts can be worth 20 to 100 times what was paid. Universa reports a 105% average annual return on the capital it actually spent on options from 2008 to 2019, audited by EY. In March 2020 its hedges returned over 4,000%. Bill Ackman turned $27 million of credit protection into $2.6 billion in three weeks.

The catch. It loses a little almost every year, so it only works as a small slice of a larger portfolio. Universa's own example: 3.33% in its fund and 96.67% in the S&P 500 compounded at 11.5% a year from 2008. Most investors cannot stomach years of steady losses waiting for the crash.

4 · Trading one volatility against another (relative value)

Capstone · bank derivatives desks · multi-strategy hedge funds

What they do. Instead of betting on whether markets will be calm or wild, they bet that one option is too expensive relative to another, and hold both sides so the overall market direction barely matters. Common versions:

  • Dispersion. Sell options on the index, buy options on the stocks inside it. Index options price in stocks moving together; when they move separately, the stock options pay more than the index options cost. Trade press says this trade grew five-fold in four years to about $1 billion of exposure per volatility point, and became crowded when the S&P 500's implied correlation hit near-record lows around 7%.
  • Calendar trades. Sell near-month options and buy later-month options when the near month looks too expensive, or the reverse.
  • Skew trades. Sell expensive downside puts and buy cheaper upside calls, or the reverse, when protection is priced unusually high.
  • Volatility arbitrage. Buy an option, then trade the underlying continuously to cancel its direction, so the only remaining bet is whether actual movement beats what the option priced.

Who. Capstone Investment Advisors, a specialist, managed about $8.7 billion in 2021 and is listed at about $13 billion in 2026. Large banks run dispersion books for their clients.

What it takes. Accurate prices for hundreds of option series, cheap execution on many legs, and the ability to hedge during the day. Daily data alone cannot do most of these.

Sources: Risk.net; Hedgeweek; Wikipedia, Capstone; PSERS document.

5 · Exploiting how the market is built

Large, and sometimes crosses legal linesExpiry-day flows · index rebalances · large orders

What it is. Knowing where predictable, forced trades will land, and positioning before them. Examples: buying or selling around index rebalances, around the huge expiry-day option activity in India, or around very large known orders.

The Indian example. SEBI's July 2025 interim order says Jane Street made ₹44,358 crore in Indian index options while losing money in the stocks and futures it traded on the same days, and that the stock and futures trades were used to move the index in a direction that helped its option positions on expiry days. SEBI impounded ₹4,843 crore. Jane Street disputes the findings. Whatever the outcome, it shows how much money sat in India's weekly expiry structure, and why SEBI cut weekly contracts in November 2024.

Lesson for us. Any historical study of Bank Nifty expiry days from 2023 to 2025 is contaminated by this activity.

Sources: Business Standard; Kotak Securities.

What about expert individual traders? They exist, but there is no reliable count. In India, about 1% of individuals made over ₹1 lakh net in FY22 to FY24, and only 13% of individuals used any algorithm. The individuals who succeed usually run a small version of model 2 (selling defined-risk premium with strict sizing) or model 3 (occasional event bets), and treat it as a business with costs measured in rupees per trade. They do not out-trade the firms in models 1, 4 and 5.

Where Alpha Signal fits

  • The project already runs model 2. The NIFTY strangle paper trade has earned 24.6% a year on margin in its backtest. That is the same business as the option-income funds, done weekly on NIFTY.
  • Model 3 is the natural partner. A small, steady budget for event straddles or far-out puts would lose a little most months and pay off in exactly the weeks the strangle suffers. That pairing is what Universa recommends to its own investors.
  • Model 4 is the realistic stretch. NIFTY calendar and skew trades can be tested on stored daily prices back to 2019. Dispersion needs the stock-option backfill and higher margin.
  • Models 1 and 5 are out of reach by design. They are where most of the money in India went, and they need speed and scale this project will never have.

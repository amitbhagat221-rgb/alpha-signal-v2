India on one page

the big picture
If you read only one card, read this one.
In one breath. India is a large, young economy that runs mostly on its own households' spending. It imports most of its energy, so oil is its weak spot. It earns dollars from services and from workers abroad, so it can usually pay the bill. More and more of its savings now flow into the stock market every month.
  1. It runs on home demand.
    Household spending is the largest part of the economy by a wide margin. Exports matter far less than in East Asian economies. That shelters India from a global slowdown, but not from global money: when foreign investors leave, Indian markets fall even if Indian demand is fine.
  2. Oil and the dollar are its two pressure points.
    India imports most of the crude it uses, plus a lot of gold and electronics. So it runs a current-account deficit, and foreign money has to fill the gap. A costly barrel or a strong dollar squeezes that gap from both sides at once.
  3. Services and remittances are its cushion.
    Software, consulting and the global capability centres earn dollars every month, and India receives more money from workers abroad than any other country. Together they pay for a large share of the oil bill. That is why a normal oil price is manageable and a shock is not.
  4. The state has been the main investor.
    Since the pandemic the government has spent heavily on roads, railways, power and defence while many private companies waited. Whether private companies now take over that role is the central question of this cycle.
  5. Inflation is about food.
    Food is a large part of what a typical Indian household buys, far more than in rich countries. So the monsoon is a macro variable, and the RBI watches vegetable prices as closely as oil.
  6. The RBI manages two things.
    It targets inflation, but it also manages the rupee. It rarely lets the currency fall freely and prefers to spend its reserves to slow a slide. Defending the rupee has side effects: it drains cash from banks and can force rates up.
  7. Savings are moving into markets.
    Indian households used to keep their savings in gold, property and bank deposits. Since the pandemic a steady stream goes into mutual funds every month through systematic investment plans. That domestic money now often absorbs foreign selling, which changes how deep a sell-off can go.
  8. Growth is priced richly.
    India has been among the fastest-growing large economies for years, and its stock market usually trades at a premium to other emerging markets. Good news is partly in the price; disappointments are punished.
Analogy. India is a big joint family that earns most of its income at home, runs its kitchen on imported gas, and has relatives abroad who send money home every month. In a normal year the remittances pay the gas bill. In a year the gas price doubles, the family dips into savings and borrows, and the neighbours start asking whether it can pay.
So what for the stockThree questions explain most of what Indian markets do in any month: what oil is doing, what the US is doing to the dollar and yields, and whether domestic money is still coming in. The rest of this page is a deeper answer to those three.
M1

The plumbing

India · how money flows
Where does India's money come from, where does it go, and where can the pipes burst?
In one breath. Households save, banks and funds pool those savings, the government and companies borrow them, and foreign money fills whatever is left over. The RBI sits on top and controls the price, the volume and the exchange rate of the money in the pipes.

Households save

  • Bank deposits
  • Monthly investment plans into mutual funds
  • Insurance and pension funds
  • Gold and property

Banks and markets pool it

  • Banks turn deposits into loans
  • Funds buy shares and bonds: these are the DIIs
  • Insurers buy long government bonds

It becomes spending

  • The government borrows first, for its deficit
  • Companies borrow for plants and working capital
  • Households borrow for homes, cars and phones

The world closes the loop

  • The current-account deficit is the shortfall
  • FDI fills it slowly and stays
  • FIIs fill it quickly and can leave
  • If they fall short, reserves drop and the rupee weakens
  1. Households save, and how they save is changing.
    Indian households save a large share of what they earn. For generations that went into gold, land and bank deposits. Since the pandemic a growing share goes into mutual funds through monthly plans, which arrive whatever the headlines say. At the same time households have borrowed more for homes and consumption, so their net financial savings have thinned. Less spare saving means more reliance on foreign money to fund investment.
  2. Banks and funds turn savings into loans and bond purchases.
    Banks lend to companies and households. Mutual funds, insurers and pension funds buy shares and bonds; together they are the domestic institutional investors. Banks are also required by rule to hold a slice of their deposits in government bonds, which gives the government a captive buyer.
  3. The government borrows first.
    The centre and the states together borrow a large part of the country's financial savings every year to fund their deficits. When they borrow more, they compete with companies for the same pool, and borrowing costs rise for everyone. Economists call that crowding out. It is why the size of the deficit matters to a stock investor.
  4. The world fills the gap.
    India invests more than it saves. The difference shows up as a current-account deficit, and it has to be financed from abroad. Foreign direct investment builds factories and rarely leaves. Foreign portfolio investors buy listed shares and bonds and can sell in a day. Loans from abroad and deposits from Indians living overseas make up the rest. When these flows fall short, the RBI sells dollars from its reserves and the rupee slides.
  5. The RBI controls the valves.
    It sets the price of money through the policy rate. It sets the volume through the cash banks must park with it and through buying or selling bonds. It sets the pace of the rupee by buying or selling dollars. These valves are connected: when the RBI sells dollars to defend the rupee, it takes rupees out of the banking system at the same time, so a currency defence tightens money even without a rate change.
FIIs and DIIs sit inside this loop, not beside it. FII money arrives when the gap needs filling and leaves when US yields rise or the rupee looks weak. DII money is Indian savings arriving every month. Over the last twenty sessions FIIs sold −₹82,020 cr and DIIs bought +₹87,231 cr: almost a mirror image.

Where the pipes burst

The plumbing has failed in recognisable ways. In 2013 foreign money left faster than the gap could be closed and the rupee crashed. In 2018 a large lender that borrowed short and lent long ran out of funding, and credit to property and autos froze for a year. In 2022 foreign investors sold heavily as US rates rose, and domestic funds stepped in for the first time on a large scale. Each failure starts at a different pipe, but each shows up first in the same three places: the rupee, short-term interest rates and bank shares.

The twin deficits and the rupee

India · the chain that matters most
Why do the oil price, the rupee, inflation and RBI rates move together, and how does it end?
In one breath. A costly barrel widens the gap foreign money has to fill. The rupee weakens, imports get dearer, inflation rises, and the RBI has to choose between defending growth and defending the currency. The government faces its own version of the same choice.
Oil jumps›Import bill rises›Current-account gap widens›Rupee weakens›Imported inflation›RBI hikes and sells dollars›Reserves fall, loans dearer›FIIs sell
  1. Oil jumps.
    India buys most of its crude abroad and pays in dollars. A higher price is effectively a tax paid to foreign producers, and it arrives every single day.
  2. The import bill and the gap widen.
    Exports do not rise to match, so the current-account deficit gets bigger. Gold and electronics imports add to it, and a strong festive season makes it worse.
  3. The rupee weakens.
    More dollars are needed than are coming in. Importers rush to buy dollars before the rupee falls further, exporters wait to sell theirs, and the move feeds on itself.
  4. Inflation rises, twice.
    Fuel, fertiliser and freight get dearer directly, and a weaker rupee makes every other import dearer too. Fuel prices are partly set by the government, so the pass-through comes in steps rather than all at once.
  5. The RBI responds.
    It raises rates to cool demand and make rupee assets more attractive, and it sells dollars to slow the slide. Both tighten money for the whole economy.
  6. Foreign investors move first.
    They watch the same chain. A weaker rupee eats into their returns, so they sell before the chain plays out, which pushes the rupee down further. Domestic funds usually buy what they sell.

What the government can do: the second deficit

The government's main tool is fuel tax. Cutting it shields pump prices and inflation, but the lost revenue widens the fiscal deficit. That is the second deficit in "twin deficits": an oil shock widens the gap with the world and the gap in the budget at the same time.

How it ends

There are three exits. The good one: oil falls back, usually because the supply problem is fixed. The slow one: higher rates and prices cool demand until imports shrink and the gap closes, at the cost of a growth slowdown. The bad one: reserves run low before either happens, the rupee drops sharply, and the RBI is forced into large rate rises. India has had all three; the 1991 crisis was the bad exit in its most extreme form.

The engines

India · what makes it grow
What drives each engine, what stops it, how it reaches stocks, and where each one stands today.
In one breath. Five engines pull the economy: household consumption, government investment, private investment, services exports and goods exports. They rarely all run at once, and the market rewards the industries attached to whichever engine is speeding up.

Consumption the largest engine by far

drives itJobs and wages, rural income from the monsoon and crop prices, cheap credit, tax cuts that leave money in pockets, festivals and weddings.
kills itFood inflation, rate rises, weak rains, a jobs scare in the cities, a squeeze on unsecured lending.
how it reaches stocksThrough the sales of companies that sell to households: two-wheelers and cars, consumer staples, paints, retail, hotels, airlines, and the lenders who finance them. Premium and mass-market demand have moved differently for several years: the top end has been strong, the bottom end weak.
where it standsFestive demand is strong and led by premium goods. Rural demand is clouded by weak rains. The income-tax and goods and services tax cuts of the past year help; the October rate rise and higher fuel prices hurt.

How the engines pass the baton

After the pandemic the government carried growth with heavy public investment while consumers recovered and companies waited. The plan was a hand-off: public capex would fill order books, factories would fill up, and private companies would start building. That hand-off has happened only in pockets. Consumption has split in two, with the well-off spending freely and the mass market struggling, which the income-tax and goods and services tax cuts of the past year were meant to fix. Exports were the hoped-for third leg and have run into tariffs. The oil shock now hits the two engines that were meant to accelerate next: consumption, through prices and rates, and private capex, through costlier money.

So what for the stockAsk which engine is speeding up, not how fast the economy is growing. In a quarter where government capex accelerates and consumption slows, construction and capital-goods stocks can rise while consumer stocks fall, even with the same headline growth number.

The referees

India · who sets the rules
Who controls what, how each one usually behaves, how to read their signals, and what they will likely do next.
In one breath. Five referees shape the macro game: the RBI sets the price of money, the Finance Ministry sets taxes and spending, the GST Council sets indirect taxes, SEBI sets market rules, and the states run their own budgets. Each has a recognisable way of reacting.

Reserve Bank of India

ControlsThe policy rate, banks' cash rules, liquidity, the rupee through its reserves, the rules for banks and lenders.
How it behavesA monetary policy committee of six sets the rate, three from the RBI and three from outside, with the governor holding the casting vote. It reacts to where it expects inflation to be a year or so out, not to this month's print. It cares about the rupee's speed more than its level, and it prefers small, signalled steps over surprises.
How to read itRead the statement for changes in wording: the stance, the inflation forecast and the vote split. A dissent is often the first sign of the next move. The governor's press conference matters as much as the decision.
LatestUnder the governor appointed in late 2024 the RBI cut rates through 2025 as inflation fell to multi-year lows. In October it reversed course: its first rate rise in years, plus a higher cash reserve rule, a dollar window for state oil firms and tighter hedging rules.
Next moveAnother rate rise: likely while oil stays above a hundred and the rupee is under pressure
Would surpriseA pause while oil is still high would surprise the market and lift rate-sensitive stocks.

Finance Ministry and the Budget

ControlsTaxes, spending, government borrowing, fuel excise duty, the sale of state companies.
How it behavesProtects capital spending above almost everything else. Has moved its fiscal target toward a debt anchor rather than a yearly number. Cuts fuel excise in oil shocks to shield pump prices, and prefers tax relief for the middle class over big new welfare schemes.
How to read itThe February Budget sets the year: capex, the deficit, borrowing and tax changes. Between budgets, watch excise decisions on fuel and the monthly tax-collection numbers.
LatestRaised the income-tax exemption sharply in the 2025 Budget and cut goods and services tax rates to fewer slabs later that year, both meant to lift consumption.
Next moveFuel-duty relief: possible if crude stays high; a capex cut before the Budget is unlikely
Would surpriseA cut in the capex target would hit construction and capital-goods stocks hard.

GST Council

ControlsIndirect tax rates and rules, decided by the centre and all the states together.
How it behavesMoves by consensus and slowly. Prefers rule changes to rate changes, and avoids anything that cuts states' revenue without compensation.
How to read itWatch the agenda before each meeting: rate changes on specific goods move individual industries overnight.
LatestApproved a big round of easier rules with no rate changes: faster refunds, simpler filing for small sellers, export status for contract manufacturing.
Next moveRate changes: unlikely in the near term
Would surpriseA surprise rate cut on a mass-market item would lift that industry's stocks.

SEBI

ControlsMarket rules, derivatives, disclosures, fund rules, foreign investor registration.
How it behavesTightens when retail speculation runs hot, consults before it acts, and phases big changes in over months.
How to read itConsultation papers signal the direction months ahead. Final rules usually soften the draft.
LatestTightened derivatives rules in late 2024. A new round of derivatives rules is due next.
Next moveTighter derivatives rules: likely
Would surpriseHits exchange, broker and depository earnings; matters little to the wider economy.

The states

ControlsTheir own budgets, power distribution companies, stamp duty on property, fuel and liquor taxes.
How it behavesSpend on welfare before elections. State borrowing is now about as large as the centre's. Power-distribution losses are the chronic weak spot.
How to read itWatch state budgets and election manifestos for cash-transfer promises, and power-distribution dues for the health of power companies.
LatestSeveral states face elections in the coming year, and Uttar Pradesh, the largest, votes in early 2027.
Next moveNew welfare promises: likely ahead of state polls
Would surpriseLarge promises in a big state widen its deficit and can lift consumption locally.
So what for the stockThe market moves on surprises, not decisions. Knowing a referee's usual reaction lets you tell a surprise from a decision that was already priced in. When the RBI does what it signalled, rate-sensitive stocks barely move; when it does more, they fall hard.

The political economy

India · what the government wants and does
What this government says it wants, what it has actually done, how coalition politics changes that, and what follows.
In one breath. A government that believes growth comes from public investment, manufacturing and digital delivery of welfare, now governing with allies. It tends to protect capex and fiscal credibility, and to deliver relief through tax cuts and transfers rather than big new spending schemes.

Who governs and how

A coalition led by the BJP has governed since 2014 and is in its third term. Since the 2024 election it relies on allies for a majority, chiefly two regional parties from Andhra Pradesh and Bihar. The alliance's strong result in the Bihar election in late 2025 steadied the coalition. Allies with state-level priorities pull spending toward their states and make reforms that hurt their voters harder to pass.

How politics moves markets

Indian markets price political risk in bursts. The clearest recent example is the 2024 election result: the market fell sharply on the day the ruling party lost its single-party majority, then recovered within days when it became clear policy would continue. State elections matter less to the index but more to specific industries: cash-transfer promises lift local consumption and widen state deficits; power-sector promises hurt power-distribution finances. Policy continuity is the asset the market pays for; uncertainty about it is what gets sold.

History that still bites

India · nine episodes
Which past episodes explain how the market reacts today, and how closely does each one rhyme with now?
In one breath. Most of what Indian markets do in a crisis has happened before. The 2013 and 2022 episodes, an oil or dollar shock meeting a wide current-account gap, rhyme most closely with today.
1991
The balance-of-payments crisis rhymes with today: strong
What happened. Oil spiked during the Gulf war, remittances from the Gulf stopped, and India ran out of foreign currency; it pledged its gold to pay for imports.
What policy did. The crisis forced the reforms that opened the economy: the rupee was devalued, licences scrapped, foreign investment allowed.
Lesson for today. Reserves and the current account are the real constraint. Watch them every time oil spikes.
2008
The global credit freeze rhymes with today: weak
What happened. Foreign investors pulled money out of every emerging market at once. The rupee fell and Indian shares more than halved from their peak.
What policy did. The RBI cut rates hard and flooded the system with money; the government spent. India recovered faster than most.
Lesson for today. In a global panic India falls with the world first and recovers on domestic demand later.
2013
The taper tantrum rhymes with today: strong
What happened. A hint that the Fed would slow its bond buying sent foreign money out of Indian bonds. With high inflation and a wide current-account gap, India was named one of the most fragile economies.
What policy did. A new governor raised rates, curbed gold imports and opened a special deposit scheme for Indians abroad that pulled in dollars fast.
Lesson for today. A wide current-account deficit plus tighter US money is India's most dangerous mix.
2016
Demonetisation rhymes with today: weak
What happened. Most of the cash in circulation was cancelled overnight. The informal economy and rural demand took the hit.
What policy did. Digital payments jumped; formal, listed companies gained share from informal rivals over the following years.
Lesson for today. Policy shocks hurt the informal economy first; listed companies often gain share afterwards.
2017
The goods and services tax rhymes with today: weak
What happened. A single national tax replaced a tangle of state and central taxes. The switch-over was messy for small firms.
What policy did. After a few painful quarters, tax collection rose and more business moved into the formal economy.
Lesson for today. Structural reforms cost a few quarters before they pay.
2018
IL&FS rhymes with today: medium
What happened. A large infrastructure lender defaulted. Shadow banks that funded themselves with short-term money could no longer borrow, and credit to property and autos froze.
What policy did. The government took over the lender; the RBI tightened rules for shadow banks. Several lenders failed over the next two years.
Lesson for today. Watch lenders that live on short-term wholesale borrowing whenever rates rise.
2020
Covid rhymes with today: weak
What happened. A sharp crash in the spring, then one of the strongest rallies on record, fuelled by cheap money and a wave of new retail investors.
What policy did. The RBI cut rates to record lows and pumped in liquidity; the government focused on capex and free grain rather than cash handouts.
Lesson for today. Central-bank liquidity moves valuations more than earnings in the short run.
2022
The inflation spike rhymes with today: strong
What happened. The Ukraine war sent oil and food prices up. US rates rose fast and foreign investors sold Indian shares heavily.
What policy did. The RBI raised rates quickly, starting between scheduled meetings. Domestic funds absorbed the foreign selling.
Lesson for today. Domestic monthly-investment money now offsets foreign selling. You can see it again in today's flows.
2024
The election-result shock rhymes with today: medium
What happened. The ruling party lost its single-party majority. The market fell sharply on results day.
What policy did. Within days it was clear the coalition would continue the same policies, and the market recovered fully.
Lesson for today. The market sells policy uncertainty, then buys continuity. Political shocks without policy change fade fast.
So what for the stockWhen today's chain matches a past episode, the past episode is the best guide to which stocks fell first and which recovered first. In 2013 and 2022 the rupee and bank shares led the fall, exporters held up, and the recovery started when the oil or dollar pressure eased, not when growth data improved.

How a global shock reaches an Indian stock

the world · walk-through
Take today's shock, the Gulf oil disruption, and follow it from a tanker to a share price.
In one breath. A disruption in the Gulf becomes a higher pump price, a weaker rupee, a rate rise and a foreign sell-off, in that order, and each step has its own winners and losers on the Indian market.
  1. The shipping route closes.
    Attacks on Gulf shipping and the closure of the Hormuz route remove supply. Brent is at 109.69, +38.22% in three months. Card W5.
  2. India's oil companies feel it first.
    State oil marketing companies buy crude at the new price but sell petrol and diesel at prices the government watches closely, so their margins shrink before anyone else's. Airlines pay more for fuel at once. Upstream producers gain unless the government taxes the windfall. Industry pages: Oil & Gas, Aviation.
  3. The government chooses.
    Either pump prices rise, which feeds inflation, or fuel taxes are cut, which widens the deficit. Card M4, Finance Ministry.
  4. Inflation picks it up.
    Fuel, freight and fertiliser costs flow into prices with a lag. CPI was +4.82% in 2026-08, up from +4.45%. Lesson 3.
  5. The rupee and the RBI respond.
    The import bill widens the current-account gap, the rupee comes under pressure, and the RBI raises rates and sells dollars. That happened in October. Card M2.
  6. Foreign investors sell.
    FIIs sold −₹82,020 cr over twenty sessions and hold only 11.3% of their index futures long. Domestic funds bought almost as much. The clock's market gauges.
  7. The stock market sorts the winners and losers.
    Paints, airlines, autos and consumer staples lose on input costs and rates. Property and shadow banks lose on rates. IT and pharma exporters gain from a weaker rupee. Upstream oil gains on price. The pulse shows which industries each day's move helps or hurts.

How to read the quadrant

The quadrant does not ask whether growth or inflation is high. It asks which way each one is moving, because markets price change, not levels. How to read the rate cycle

Rate cycles are long. Central banks move in small steps and rarely reverse quickly, so the first move in a new direction matters more than any later one: it tells you the regime has changed. A first rise after a run of cuts, like October's, is rare and usually comes only when the central bank sees a threat it cannot ignore, here the rupee and imported inflation. The question now is whether it was a one-off insurance move or the start of a series. The wording of the next statement and the vote split will tell you.

Who feels tighter money first

  1. Lenders that borrow short.
    Shadow banks and housing finance companies that fund themselves in the money market pay more within weeks.
  2. Banks, in two directions.
    Loans reprice upward, which helps margins at first; the higher cash reserve rule and slower loan growth hurt. Banks with cheap, sticky deposits come out ahead.
  3. Borrowers and buyers.
    Home buyers, car buyers and companies with heavy debt see their costs rise over the following quarters.
  4. Valuations.
    Higher rates lower the value of future profits, which hits richly priced growth stocks hardest.
So what for the stockIn a tightening cycle, prefer lenders with cheap deposits over lenders that borrow in the market, and companies with net cash over companies with heavy debt. The industry pages for Banks, NBFCs, Real Estate Developers and Automobiles each list the rate as one of their dials.

How to read each gauge

  1. FII index-futures long share.
    Foreign investors hold both long and short positions in Nifty futures. The share that is long tells you how they are betting on the index. When almost all of it is short, they have already sold the bad news. Shorts have to be bought back eventually, so a crowded short is fuel for a sharp rally when the news turns. It is a contrarian gauge at the extremes and noise in the middle.
  2. FII and DII cash flows.
    The bars show who bought and who sold Indian shares each day. Since the pandemic the two have often been mirror images: foreign money sells, domestic monthly-investment money buys. A day when foreign investors buy after a long selling run is worth noting, but one day is not a trend. Lesson 7 explains how not to over-read them.
  3. India VIX.
    The price of protection on the Nifty, in volatility terms. It rises when investors pay up for insurance. A middling VIX during heavy foreign selling means the market is absorbing the selling without panic.
  4. Copper against gold.
    Copper goes into wires, buildings and machines, so it rises when growth looks strong. Gold rises when investors want safety. Copper beating gold means markets still expect growth.
  5. Brent, year on year.
    How much more oil costs than a year ago. A rising year-on-year number is what feeds through into inflation over the following months.
So what for the stockGauges tell you what is already priced in. When the data is bad but positioning is extremely bearish, the next surprise is more likely to push prices up than down. That is why a crowded foreign short sits on the risk radar as amber, not red.

What the reflation row says, with care

In past reflation months metals, energy, infrastructure and realty tended to beat the Nifty, while gold, pharma and FMCG tended to lag. That fits the textbook: in a growth-and-inflation regime, companies that sell commodities or build things gain pricing power, and defensive or safe-haven assets lose favour. Two caveats. Today's reflation comes with an oil supply shock, which is worse for oil consumers than a demand-led reflation. And realty's past strength came in months when rates were low; this time the RBI is hiking.

The Indian twists

  1. Gold is priced in dollars but bought in rupees.
    A weaker rupee lifts the rupee price of gold even when the dollar price is flat, so Indian gold behaves better in a rupee crisis than the textbook suggests.
  2. Government bonds have a new buyer.
    Indian bonds joined global bond indices in 2024, so foreign index funds now buy them steadily. That softens the sell-off in bonds when rates rise, but it also means foreign selling can hit bonds as well as shares in a crisis.
  3. Cash pays more than it used to.
    With the RBI hiking, short-term deposits and liquid funds earn more, which makes waiting less costly than in a low-rate year.
  4. Equities are not one asset.
    In India the gap between sectors in a regime is often larger than the move in the index. The regime table above is more useful than the equity column here.
Our own history disagrees with the textbook in places: gold lagged the Nifty in reflation months in the regime table. The playbook phase replaces these words with the measured table.

Lesson 3 · Read a CPI release

worked example on our data
The consumer price index is the price of a fixed basket a typical household buys. The RBI targets its year-on-year change.
  1. Start with the headline.
    CPI rose +4.82% year on year in 2026-08, up from +4.45% the month before. The RBI targets four percent with a tolerance band around it, so this is above the middle and rising. The direction matters as much as the level: the RBI acts on where it thinks inflation is heading.
  2. Then food.
    Food and beverages rose +5.66%, up from +5.25%. Food is close to half the Indian basket, far more than in rich countries, so a bad monsoon or a vegetable spike can move the headline on its own. The RBI usually looks through a food spike it thinks is temporary and acts when it spreads to other prices.
  3. Then fuel and housing.
    Housing and fuel rose +2.61%, up from +2.15%. Pump prices are partly set by the government, so an oil shock reaches CPI slowly and in steps, often after a tax decision. That is why this line is still low while Brent has jumped: the pass-through is still to come.
  4. Then core.
    Core inflation strips out food and fuel to show the underlying trend: whether price rises are spreading into services, rents and manufactured goods. We do not hold the core series yet, which is why the clock cannot yet say whether inflation is broadening.
  5. Finally, the base effect.
    Year-on-year inflation compares this month with the same month last year. If prices jumped a year ago, this year's number looks low even if prices are rising now, and the reverse. Always check what happened twelve months earlier.

Lesson 7 · Read FII and DII flows

worked example on our data
Foreign and domestic institutions report what they bought and sold every day. The numbers are useful and easy to over-read.
  1. Know what the number is.
    On 2026-10-16 foreign investors were net buyers of +₹1,166 cr in the cash market and domestic institutions net sellers of −₹3,661 cr. These are provisional figures for the cash market only; derivatives positions are separate.
  2. Never read one day.
    One buying day after weeks of selling is noise. Over twenty sessions foreign investors sold −₹82,020 cr and domestic funds bought +₹87,231 cr. The trend is what matters.
  3. Look at both together.
    When foreign selling is matched by domestic buying, the index falls slowly and recovers quickly. When both sell, the fall is fast. Since the pandemic, monthly household investment has made domestic buying the norm.
  4. Add positioning.
    Cash flows say what investors did; futures positions say what they are betting. Foreign investors hold 11.3% of their index futures long, the 12th percentile since 2022. Heavy cash selling plus an extreme short says the bad news is largely sold already.
  5. Ask why they are selling.
    Foreign investors sell India for global reasons as often as Indian ones: higher US yields, a stronger dollar, a better opportunity elsewhere. Check the US card before concluding that foreign investors have turned against India.
Exercise.
  1. Find the last day in the flows chart where both foreign and domestic investors sold. What did the Nifty do that week?
  2. If foreign investors cover their futures shorts, which large-cap industries would you expect to rise first, and why?

Lesson 10 · Read oil and the rupee together

worked example on our data
The twin-deficit chain from card M2, read off live numbers.
  1. Start with oil, in three windows.
    Brent is 109.69: +3.56% today, +10.52% in a month, +38.22% in three months. The three-month change is what moves the import bill and inflation; the daily change moves sentiment.
  2. Then the rupee.
    USD/INR is 94.95, -0.46% over three months, below its record of 96.88 on 2026-07-24. Oil up sharply with the rupee roughly flat means something is holding the rupee: here, the RBI selling dollars and its new defences.
  3. Then ask what is holding the line.
    If the RBI is holding the rupee, reserves are falling and money is tightening. That is not free: it moves the pressure from the currency to interest rates and liquidity, which is why the RBI also raised rates.
  4. Then the second deficit.
    Check whether the government is cutting fuel taxes. If it is, inflation stays lower but the fiscal deficit widens and bond yields rise. If it is not, pump prices and inflation rise.
  5. Finally, the stocks.
    Map each step to industries: oil consumers lose on cost, rate-sensitive borrowers lose on rates, exporters gain only if the rupee actually weakens. Today the rupee is being held, so exporters are getting less help than the oil price alone would suggest.
Exercise.
  1. If the RBI stopped defending the rupee tomorrow, which way would the rupee move, and which three industries would gain?
  2. Using the risk radar, which reading would tell you first that reserves are running low?

Glossary

every term on this page
One line each, plain words.
Current-account deficit
India buys more goods and services from the world than it sells; foreign money has to fill the gap.
Twin deficits
A current-account deficit and a fiscal deficit at the same time; an oil shock widens both.
FII, FPI
Foreign institutional or portfolio investors: overseas funds buying Indian shares and bonds.
FDI
Foreign direct investment: a foreign company building or buying a business in India; it rarely leaves quickly.
DII
Domestic institutional investors: Indian mutual funds, insurers and pension funds.
Systematic investment plan
A fixed monthly investment into a mutual fund; the steady stream behind domestic buying.
Repo rate
The rate at which the RBI lends to banks overnight; the policy rate everything else follows.
Cash reserve ratio
The share of deposits banks must park with the RBI, earning nothing. Raising it drains money from the system.
Liquidity
How much spare cash banks have; tight liquidity pushes short-term rates up even without a policy change.
Crowding out
Government borrowing taking savings that companies would otherwise have borrowed, pushing rates up.
Forex reserves
The RBI's holdings of dollars and other foreign assets; its ammunition for defending the rupee.
IIP
Index of industrial production: monthly output of factories, mines and power plants.
CPI, core CPI
Consumer prices; core removes food and fuel to show the underlying trend.
Base effect
A year-on-year number looking high or low because of what happened twelve months earlier.
Regime quadrant
Growth speeding up or slowing, against inflation rising or falling: four states with different winners.
Reflation, stagflation
Growth and inflation both rising; inflation rising while growth slows.
σ (sigma)
How many typical daily moves today's move was. Two σ happens roughly one day in twenty.
t-statistic
How far an average is from zero in units of its own noise. Our bar for a factor is 2.5 before the multiple-testing haircut.
Crowded short
Most traders already bet on a fall, so there are few sellers left and good news forces them to buy back.
Carry trade
Borrowing in a cheap currency such as the yen to buy higher-yielding assets; it unwinds violently when the cheap currency jumps.
Taper tantrum
The 2013 sell-off in emerging markets after the Fed hinted it would slow its bond buying.
Carbon border tax
An EU charge on the carbon in imported goods such as steel, aluminium, cement and fertiliser.
